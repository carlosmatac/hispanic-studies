### Editorial spine: Roman Hispania, the Reconquista and the oceanic turn

**The opening date is 218 BCE.** Begin with Rome’s entry into Iberia during the Second Punic War. The milestones of conquest and provincial incorporation give the Atlas the first political and geographical setting for the history of Hispanic and Portuguese civilization. Common inheritances such as Latin, civic institutions, Roman law and Christianity were forged in stages. The medieval project Bueno associates with Asturias is a later turning point, separate from the Roman beginning.

**Iberia was a peninsula of kingdoms.** The Reconquista is a long interaction of fronts, conquests, resettlement, legal incorporation and dynastic consolidation. A Castilian–Leonese union does not erase Galicia, León or the later Andalusian kingdoms. The Crown of Aragón distinguishes Aragón, Catalan counties and the later Principality, Mallorca and Valencia. Navarre and Portugal have lines of their own. The Atlas retains Muslim polities as historical actors, including the taifa kingdoms and the emirate of Granada. The Atlas explains the Catholic–Islamic geopolitical confrontation while identifying the particular states, local agreements and cross-confessional alliances actually involved.

**Bueno’s geopolitical thesis.** In [“La Teoría de la Esfera y el Descubrimiento de América,” section III](https://filosofia.org/rev/bas/bas20101.htm), Bueno explicitly includes among the Crown’s political objectives a route by which the Turks could be caught from behind. His [“España”](https://filosofia.org/rev/bas/bas22403.htm) links this to the long struggle with Islamic powers in Iberia. He calls the process *recubrimiento*: Christian Iberian polities, in his account, could not ensure their survival by remaining behind a static frontier and had to project power beyond it. The sphericity of the Earth made a western approach to Asia conceivable. He reads Magellan–Elcano as the practical demonstration of the global geometry that made the plan intelligible. The 1492 crossing is thus the opening of an unexpected American outcome within an expedition conceived in relation to the Indies.

**Put the thesis on the map without confusing the record types.** Show the fall of Constantinople in 1453, the Granada surrender and the [Capitulations of Santa Fe](https://patrimoni.gencat.cat/en/catalunyapaisdarxius/digital-resource/resource/document/capitulacions-de-santa-fe-acordades-entre-els-reis-catolics-i-cristofor-colom-sobre-el-seu-projectat-viatge-a-les-indies/) in 1492, and the actual Caribbean landfalls as factual nodes. Label the hypothetical westward approach to the Ottoman rear “Geopolitical interpretation: Gustavo Bueno”; the royal instrument does not state an operational order to attack the Ottomans. Trade with Asia was neither fully closed nor governed by a single Islamic power. Place Portugal’s Cape route on a parallel track, pursued through the Atlantic islands, the African coast and Indian Ocean trade networks. Ottoman expansion, Catholic universal and evangelizing aims, Portugal–Castile rivalry, commercial calculation and cosmography belong in the same account.

### 2. What an unlock means

Use **“Recorded on the selected map”** for the game mechanic. A cell can be inhabited, well known locally, misdrawn on a printed map and administered by a different polity at the same time. In playback those layers can disagree. The [1562 Gutiérrez chart](https://www.loc.gov/item/map49000970/) and [1627 Albernaz atlas](https://lhs.unb.br/atlas/index.php?title=%28Albernaz%2C_1627%29) show why the renderer needs individual cartographic witnesses.

| Layer | Trigger | Render |
|---|---|---|
| Indigenous knowledge at encounter | Dated meeting, pilotage, alliance, survey, translation or later documentary transmission | Point, name, sourced segment or record at the date knowledge enters the chronology; no standalone earlier layer |
| Observation or encounter | Specific dated witness, linked to an actor | Point or uncertainty area; no hinterland reveal |
| Navigation or land transit | Log and supported legs | Route; dashed interpolation; antimeridian-safe; never a corridor of sovereignty |
| Cartography | A dated chart incorporating the feature | Coastline, river reach, place label or specific map footprint at the documented scale |
| Systematic survey | Instrumented coverage or national survey of known scale | Survey footprint and acquisition/publication dates; separate satellite imagery |
| Settlement | Founding, relocation, abandonment or change of occupation | Settlement point, or local footprint when sourced |
| Jurisdiction | Dated civil, ecclesiastical, military or judicial establishment or repeal | Validity window and typed authority; overlaps allowed |
| Claims and borders | Signed, ratified, effective or demarcated instrument | Claim and boundary layers; legal changes do not prove local implementation |
| Effective control | Evidence at the relevant time and geographic scale | Fort, occupied town, front or supported area; display independent of title |

Never fill a modern country from a sighting or a treaty synopsis. Keep a muted orientation basemap separate. Allow “no evidence in this layer” to persist through the end of an Iberian timeline; use a separate all-surveys view for later geographical coverage. Use insets or point markers for small islands and ports. Preserve settlements and Indigenous places already documented in the timeline through sovereignty changes. When a historical chart depicts a geographical error, the chart layer should allow viewers to see it.

### 5. Developer and LLM import contract

Each A-ID identifies an editorial record. For ingestion use fields: id, title by locale, date label, date precision, sort key, four paragraphs by locale, event type, regions, anchor text, Wikipedia, further reading, source status, media role, legacy aliases and child actions. This English manuscript uses locale-ready fields; preserve existing Spanish text and leave missing translations pending. Portuguese is an optional separate locale.

An approved action requires its own ID, actor, exactness, evidence, effective date or range, feature geometry provenance, operation, assertion kind, and independent date and geometry verification. The date can be no more precise than its source; the prose range is never an automatic shape validity interval. Treaty signature, ratification, effect, control and demarcation can all differ. Store overlapping civil, ecclesiastical and military jurisdictions independently. Use separate event and recorded-on dates for local knowledge, and make the recording/transmission date the earliest display date. Interpretive corridors require author, source and visible hypothesis labeling; they cannot trigger a territorial or geographic unlock. Store years-ago and BCE dates on explicit axes rather than automatically treating them as exact years.

#### A07 · 218–19 BCE · The Roman conquest of Hispania

**Type:** conflict  
**Regions:** iberia; mediterranean  
**Map anchor:** Emporion, Tarraco, Carthago Nova, Numantia and the Cantabrian mountains

The Atlas opens in 218 BCE, when Roman forces landed at Emporion during the Second Punic War. This marks the opening of Roman conquest and the start of the Atlas’s Hispanic civilizational chronology, rather than the immediate establishment of a unified Roman province.

Carthago Nova fell to Scipio in 209 BCE, and the conflict with Carthage gave way to struggles against multiple Iberian powers. The Lusitanian wars and the resistance of Viriathus, the fall of Numantia in 133 BCE, and the Augustan wars in the northwest belong to distinct theatres and generations.

Conquest established the basis for extending Roman law, Latin and urban institutions unevenly across the peninsula. Mediterranean ports and inland roads enabled the movement of troops, minerals, agricultural products and ideas. Over time these networks made Hispania a Roman political and cultural space from which later Hispanic and Lusophone institutions inherited much.

Open playback with the 218 BCE coastal arrival, not a filled modern peninsula. Add Carthago Nova, the Lusitanian campaigns, Numantia and Augustus’s Cantabrian–Asturian wars as dated child actions. Link territorial gains separately to provincial organization and later civic incorporation.

#### A113 · 197 BCE–c. 400 CE · Roman Hispania: provinces, cities, law, and Christian networks

**Type:** jurisdiction  
**Regions:** iberia  
**Map anchor:** Tarraco, Corduba, Emerita Augusta, Bracara Augusta and Carthago Nova

Rome created Hispania Citerior and Ulterior in 197 BCE while conquest was still under way. The Augustan settlement reorganized much of the peninsula into Tarraconensis, Baetica and Lusitania; later imperial reforms produced subdivisions including Gallaecia and Carthaginiensis.

Roads and ports connected mountains, mines, agricultural districts and provincial capitals. The Roman cities of Bracara and Emerita became administrative anchors, but the province of Lusitania did not have the borders of modern Portugal; neither did Roman Gallaecia coincide with the present autonomous community.

Latin spread through military, civic and commercial life and furnished the linguistic inheritance from which Iberian Romance languages developed. Christianity took root in late Roman urban communities; the early Council of Elvira is evidence of ecclesiastical organization, though the precise year remains debated.

Show dated provincial seats, road segments and evidence-based boundary intervals. Keep Roman municipal law, ecclesiastical geography and economy distinct. This is a Roman Iberian foundation for the Atlas, not an assertion that Spain and Portugal already existed.

#### A114 · 409–711 · Suevic Gallaecia and the Visigothic kingdom of Toledo

**Type:** jurisdiction  
**Regions:** iberia  
**Map anchor:** Braga, Mérida, Toledo, Cartagena and the Byzantine southeastern coast

Suevic control developed in the northwest after the early fifth-century movements into Hispania. Braga became a religious and political center with a history distinct from Toledo. The Suevic kingdom was conquered by Leovigild in 585, and should remain distinguishable on the map until its incorporation.

Visigothic authority also competed with Byzantine Spania along portions of the southern and southeastern coast. That contest, and the independent history of the northwest, make a single uninterrupted fifth-century Visigothic peninsula misleading.

#### A115 · 711–1031 · Guadalete, the conquest of Hispania and the political cycles of al-Andalus

**Type:** jurisdiction  
**Regions:** iberia; north-africa  
**Map anchor:** Strait of Gibraltar → Guadalete region → Toledo, Córdoba and Zaragoza

Ṭāriq ibn Ziyād crossed the Strait in 711 and defeated the forces of King Roderic. The site of the battle conventionally called Guadalete is disputed. Umayyad commanders then advanced by warfare and negotiated submission; the agreement associated with Theodemir illustrates an alternative to occupation by battle.

The seizure of centers including Toledo produced political rupture, while some local structures and communities persisted. The independent emirate established at Córdoba in 756, the caliphate proclaimed in 929 and its dissolution in 1031 were three different state changes.

Córdoba’s power rested on revenue, irrigated agriculture, urban craft and long-distance networks. Warfare with Christian states was recurrent, but trade, diplomacy and local agreement crossed frontiers. Almanzor’s later raids, including Santiago in 997, are expedition records rather than permanent conquests.

Represent crossing, battles, pact zones and regime changes independently. Place the disputed battlefield schematically and show the emirate, caliphate, frontier commands and ensuing taifas in distinct time slices.

#### A117 · c. 778–1137 · Pamplona and Navarre: a Pyrenean kingdom takes shape

**Type:** jurisdiction  
**Regions:** iberia  
**Map anchor:** Roncesvalles, Pamplona, Nájera, Viguera and the western Pyrenees

Roncesvalles in 778 belongs to a history of Frankish intervention and local resistance; it does not itself establish the borders of a kingdom. Pamplona’s ninth-century monarchy arose through regional forces and changing relationships with Franks, Córdoba and the Ebro valley.

Sancho III’s reach in the early eleventh century linked counties and kingdoms through kinship and temporary authority. It did not turn all lands associated with him into Navarre. Viguera’s short-lived tenth-century subkingdom illustrates why a dynastic appanage needs its own status and validity.

**Regions:** iberia; north-africa

The Almohads later took over much of the region and defeated Alfonso VIII of Castile at Alarcos in 1195. The battle and the resulting balance of power should be shown separately from wider North African chronology.

In 1212 a coalition led by the kings of Castile, Aragón and Navarre defeated the Almohad army at Las Navas de Tolosa. Papal support and military orders situate the campaign within the history of crusade; participation did not abolish the rival agendas of the Iberian crowns.

- **Iberian archival evidence:** [PARES catalogue](https://pares.cultura.gob.es/) and its [New Spain cartographic series](https://pares.cultura.gob.es/ParesBusquedas20/catalogo/description/20806/imprimir); [Biblioteca Digital Hispánica](https://bdh.bne.es/); [Biblioteca Nacional Digital de Portugal](https://bndigital.bnportugal.gov.pt/); [Arquivo Nacional da Torre do Tombo](https://digitarq.arquivos.pt/); [Atlas Digital da América Lusa](https://lhs.unb.br/atlas/).
- **Coverage and names:** [Library of Congress historical manuscript map guide](https://guides.loc.gov/luso-hispanic-world-in-maps/introduction); [David Rumsey Map Collection](https://www.davidrumsey.com/); [Historic Map Works and repository metadata via OldMapsOnline](https://www.oldmapsonline.org/); [UNESCO World Heritage List](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/); [USGS EarthExplorer](https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/). Use these as finding aids; check underlying rights and individual dates.
- **Borders and treaties:** [United Nations Treaty Collection](https://treaties.un.org/); [ICJ cases](https://www.icj-cij.org/cases); [Guadalupe Hidalgo at U.S. National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-guadalupe-hidalgo); [Gadsden Purchase](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1830-1860/gadsden-purchase); [Western Sahara at the UN](https://www.un.org/dppa/decolonization/en/nsgt/western-sahara). Historical legal titles and current disputes should be reviewed by a qualified editor before applying geometries.
- **Basemap and provenance:** [Natural Earth](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/about/terms-of-use/) for an orientation basemap; [OpenHistoricalMap](https://www.openhistoricalmap.org/) only as a reviewed geometry starting point, preserving per-feature provenance and checking license exceptions. Published national datasets and community-led research may provide more precise coverage. Never adopt a public-facing polygon for a sensitive Indigenous site merely because a coordinate can be downloaded.
